Hans Architecture

Hans is a persistent character, not a chatbot. This page shows what that persistence costs to build: which processes run where, how data flows from the camera to an answer, what happens at night, and where the gates stand that stop the model from passing a guess off as a fact.

Two machines, three processes

Hans is not one binary. On the Raspberry Pi three separate processes talk to each other over Unix sockets, and everything that needs a GPU sits on the PC behind HTTP. The split is strict: the Pi sees and decides, the PC speaks and paints.

A practical consequence that only becomes obvious after a while in the code: gestures, face recognition and guard mode work even with the PC powered off — none of them passes through a language model.

Night: where events turn into character

During the day Hans collects. At night, when it is quiet and nobody is waiting for an answer, a batch turns raw events into memory, opinions and finally identity. Every step is failure-resilient — when the "brain" is down, work is deferred and caught up rather than lost.

Two registers: why Hans doesn't make things up

A generative model likes to fill in facts. Hans doesn't fix that by pleading in the prompt, but by routing: a factual query takes a different path than a creative one. The factual register is grounded and abstains by default — it answers from data or admits it has no record. The imaginative register (dreams, paintings, musings) is free to invent. Trouble would only arise where imagination starts claiming facts.

Where the human stands

Three places where Hans deliberately does not decide alone. This isn't extra caution — they are exactly the points where a mistake would be irreversible or would quietly change who Hans is.

  • Agent actions — 23 whitelisted actions, nothing outside it; risky ones (shut down the PC, go to sleep) always confirm.
  • Identity change — Severka only proposes; until a human approves, CORE doesn't change. Who I was → am → why is versioned.
  • A new tool — when Hans finds a model for his own work, he submits it for approval; he never pulls anything himself.

Models and VRAM management

Several roles share one GPU. The chat model stays resident; the others load on demand and release VRAM after use — otherwise they would fight over it.

Game mode. When a game starts on the PC, Hans frees VRAM by himself and stops calling models. Perception, gestures and guarding keep running — that part doesn't need the GPU. It is a hard interlock: while the game runs, nothing restarts.
Privacy. The diary, face biometrics, tokens and addresses stay local and never enter git. Only the code and tuned values are public; household names, keys and IPs live in a separate file outside the repository.

Hans is a reference project tied to specific hardware (Raspberry Pi 5 + Hailo-8L, Pi camera, a PC running Ollama) — it serves as an architecture showcase rather than plug-and-play software.
